The Process

Phases of Training

01

Teaching Phase

The focus is on teaching the desired behaviours and clearly demonstrating the expectations to the dog. This foundational stage ensures the dog understands what is required.

02

Correction Phase

The dog is held accountable to the established standards. This stage emphasises teaching the dog to take responsibility for its actions, encouraging better decision-making and reinforcing the learned behaviours.

03

Conditioning Phase

Consistent practice of the learned behaviours establishes them as automatic responses. More critically, this phase aims to solidify an improved state of mind, making it the dog’s default.

04

Proofing Phase

The dog’s newly acquired behaviours and mental state are tested under various distractions and competing stimuli. This ensures the dog remains focused and compliant despite external influences.

05

Generalisation Phase

Learned behaviours are applied across diverse environmental settings. This stage ensures the dog’s obedience remains consistent and reliable in any location or situation.

06

Testing Phase

The effectiveness of the training is evaluated in real-world, everyday scenarios. Any deficiencies are identified so the training remains practical, functional and robust.

Perspective

A few words on obedience training

Many dog owners believe that obedience training is simply about teaching a few basic commands like sit, down, or come. While these behaviours are important, they are only one piece of a much larger puzzle.

True obedience and a well-balanced dog are built on four key quadrants: skills, tools, management, and mindset. When all four are addressed properly, you create a reliable, respectful, and happy companion. Neglect one, and progress becomes much harder.

1. Skills & Behaviours

Your dog needs to be taught the necessary skills required for success. Behaviours such as sit, down, stay, recall, and place must be well trained, proofed, generalised, and thoroughly tested. These behaviours need to be rock solid and reliable in real-life situations — not just in the living room when there are no distractions.

This takes time, consistency, and proper training progression. A behaviour that is only 70% reliable is not truly trained. For long-term success, these foundational skills must become automatic responses your dog can offer even under mild to moderate distractions.

2. Tools for Success

Even with strong behaviours, you need the right tools to implement and reinforce the training effectively. Surprisingly, many problems can easily be solved by simply putting a leash and collar on the dog. Tools like prong collars, e-collars, place beds, and crates can make all the difference.

The goal is not to rely on tools forever, but to use them strategically to communicate clearly and set your dog up for success while they are still learning.

3. Management & Environment

Proper management of your dog and the environment is critical. Unwanted behaviours that cannot be corrected need to be prevented — and vice versa. If you cannot correct a behaviour (for example, your dog barking while you’re away at work), then you must prevent it. If you cannot prevent a behaviour (for example, a reactive dog lunging at other dogs behind a fence), then you must correct it.

This quadrant requires the willingness to take responsibility for both your dog and the environment. The dog that cannot be trusted around cats should be kept away from cats until they can be. The best approach is often also the simplest — using crates, leashes, gates, or supervision to prevent rehearsal of unwanted behaviours.

4. Mindset & Leadership

The fourth and most important component is mindset and the correct psychological approach — in both the dog and yourself. If you cannot adopt the mindset and attitude of a calm, confident leader, your dog will never respect you enough to take anything you say or ask seriously. It is not a good idea to treat a dog as if he is weak and incapable. Treat him as if he is capable of a higher standard, and he will be.

Dogs thrive when they have clear boundaries, fair leadership, and consistent expectations. A strong mindset from the handler creates a confident, secure dog who looks to their owner for guidance rather than making their own rules.

Putting It All Together

Obedience is not one-dimensional. Real success comes when you develop all four quadrants together:

  • Strong, reliable skills
  • Effective tools to support training
  • Smart management to prevent problems
  • The right mindset and leadership

Focusing only on teaching behaviours (quadrant 1) while ignoring the others is why so many owners feel stuck despite years of training classes.

Final Thoughts

Training a dog is about much more than commands — it’s about building a relationship based on trust, respect, and clear communication. By addressing skills, tools, management, and especially mindset, you give your dog the best chance at becoming the calm, obedient companion you’ve always wanted.

Start by honestly assessing where you and your dog stand in each quadrant. Pick one area to improve this week. Small, consistent changes in all four areas will compound into lasting results over time.
